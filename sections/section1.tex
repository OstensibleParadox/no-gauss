\section{Introduction}

Which infinite-dimensional linear transformations preserve the null sets
of a product measure? Feldman--H\'ajek theory answers this question for
Gaussian laws through a condition on the covariance operators
\cite{feldman1958equivalence,hajek1958property,shepp1966radon,bogachev1998gaussian}.
Kakutani's theorem answers it when both laws are products, by multiplying
their coordinate affinities \cite{kakutani1948equivalence}. General linear
mixing of a non-Gaussian product falls outside both criteria: Gaussianity
is absent, and mixing destroys the independence needed for Kakutani's
factorization. The missing classification concerns exactly these
transformations, which mix the coordinates without either classical
structure to decide equivalence.

We give a necessary and sufficient operator criterion. For centered
non-Gaussian i.i.d.\ coordinates with finite variance, a positive density,
and finite location and scale Fisher information, a bounded invertible
linear mixing preserves the measure class if and only if it differs from
a coordinate permutation with law-preserving signs by a Hilbert--Schmidt
operator. Every other mixing produces a singular law. The transformed
coordinates may be dependent, and the operator may mix infinitely many
inputs into infinitely many outputs.

The mathematical change from the Gaussian classification is the symmetry
from which the perturbation is measured. A standard Gaussian product is
invariant under every orthogonal mixing. For a non-Gaussian product, even
preserving the measure class forces the mixing to approach the original
coordinate axes, with square-summable error. The Hilbert--Schmidt scale
survives the loss of Gaussianity; the freedom to rotate does not.

\subsection{Main theorem}

Write \(\mu=\rho^{\otimes\N}\), \(\rho(dx)=f(x)\,dx\), and \(g=\sqrt f\).
Our main assumptions are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 f>0\quad\text{Lebesgue-almost everywhere},\qquad
 g\in W^{1,2}(\R),\qquad xg'\in L^2(\R),\\
 X\sim\rho,\qquad \E X=0,\qquad \E X^2=1,\qquad
 f\ne(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/2}.
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:main-assumptions}
\end{equation}
Derivatives are weak. The two Sobolev conditions give finite location and
scale Fisher information.

Put \(H=\ell^2(\N;\R)\), let \((e_i)_{i\ge1}\) be its coordinate basis, and
write \(\mathcal S_2(H)\) for the Hilbert--Schmidt ideal~\cite{simon2005trace}.
Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P=\bigl\{Q:(Qx)_i=\varepsilon_i x_{\pi(i)},\,
       \pi\in\operatorname{Sym}(\N),\,
       \varepsilon_i\in\{\pm1\},\,
       \varepsilon_iX\sim\rho\ (i\in\N)\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Permutations always preserve the product law; reflections do so precisely
when \(\rho\) is symmetric. These are the symmetries collected in
\(\mathcal P\). For \(T\in\mathcal B(H)\), write
\(\nu_T=\Law((\sum_jT_{ij}X_j)_{i\ge1})\), where the \(X_j\) are independent
with law \(\rho\). The rows belong to \(\ell^2\), so each sum exists in
\(L^2\). We write \(\Phi_T\) for the row map; its composition law is
\eqref{eq:measurable-composition}.

To measure the accumulated change of law, we use Hellinger affinity and
distance:
\begin{equation}
 \Aff(\lambda,\nu)
 =\int\sqrt{\frac{d\lambda}{dm}\frac{d\nu}{dm}}\,dm,
 \qquad
 h^2(\lambda,\nu)=2-2\Aff(\lambda,\nu).
\end{equation}
Here \(m\) dominates both measures. With \(\mu_n=\rho^{\otimes n}\) and
\(\pi_n\) the projection onto the first \(n\) coordinates, the corresponding
energy is
\begin{equation}
 E_n(T)=-2\log\Aff\bigl(\mu_n,(\pi_n)_\#\nu_T\bigr).
 \label{eq:projected-hellinger-energy}
\end{equation}
Every input coordinate may contribute to these \(n\) outputs. Increasing
\(n\) reveals more of the change of law and can only increase the energy.

\begin{theorem}[Bounded invertible linear mixing]
\label{thm:measure-equivalence}
Under \eqref{eq:main-assumptions}, every \(T\in\GL(H)\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \nu_T\sim\mu
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 T\in\mathcal P+\mathcal S_2(H)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sup_nE_n(T)<\infty.
 }
\end{equation}
If these conditions fail, then \(\nu_T\perp\mu\) and
\(E_n(T)\uparrow\infty\).  Moreover, \(\nu_T=\mu\) if and only if
\(T\in\mathcal P\).
\end{theorem}

For the same operator \(T\) acting on a standard Gaussian sequence \(G\),
the Feldman--H\'ajek criterion reads
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \Law(TG)\sim\Law(G)
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad TT^*-I\in\mathcal S_2(H)\\
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T-U\in\mathcal S_2(H)
       \quad\text{for some orthogonal }U.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:measure-equivalence} replaces this orthogonal \(U\) by a
coordinate symmetry \(Q\in\mathcal P\). Covariance alone can no longer
decide equivalence: an orthogonal mixing still has \(TT^*=I\), but its
non-Gaussian image is singular whenever it lies outside every such
Hilbert--Schmidt neighbourhood. The new classification detects changes
that the Gaussian covariance criterion cannot see.

Kakutani's criterion encounters a different obstruction. Multiplying the
one-coordinate affinities after mixing would compare \(\mu\) with the
product of the output marginals, rather than with their joint law
\(\nu_T\). The energy \(E_n(T)\) uses the full joint distribution of the
first \(n\) outputs and retains their dependence. The theorem identifies
boundedness of these energies with a Hilbert--Schmidt condition on \(T\),
without requiring the image to factor into independent coordinates or
finite blocks.

Block rotations illustrate what Kakutani already settles. Rotating two
independent Laplace variables gives an equivalent law because both
densities are positive everywhere. Repeating a rotation through
\(\pi/4\) on disjoint pairs gives singularity by Kakutani's theorem,
applied to the pairs. For a general mixing there is no such independent
block decomposition; Theorem~\ref{thm:measure-equivalence} gives the
operator criterion in its absence.

The theorem includes Laplace and \(e^{-x^4}\) densities after normalization,
as well as asymmetric densities with an infinite fourth moment; examples
appear at the end of Section~5. Two consequences distinguish the rigidity
of exact symmetries from the regularity needed to control perturbations.

\begin{corollary}[Moment-only stabilizer]
\label{cor:moment-only-stabilizer}
If \(\rho\) is non-Gaussian, centered, and has variance one, then the exact
linear stabilizer of \(\rho^{\otimes\N}\) is \(\mathcal P\).  No density or
Fisher information assumption is needed.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Orthogonal transformations]
\label{cor:orthogonal-equivalence}
If \(f>0\) almost everywhere, \(\sqrt f\in W^{1,2}(\R)\), and \(f\) is
non-Gaussian, centered, and has variance one, then every orthogonal \(U\)
satisfies
\[
 \nu_U\sim\mu
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 U-Q\in\mathcal S_2(H)\quad\text{for some }Q\in\mathcal P.
\]
Rotations have no diagonal scale tangent, which is why location Fisher
information suffices here.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Further context}

Shimomura's Theorem~3.4 shows that \(I-S^2\) is compact for a self-adjoint
admissible operator \(S\), under \(\ell^2\)-continuity,
\(\ell^2\)-translation quasi-invariance, and \(\ell^2\)-ergodicity; his
examples preclude a universal Hilbert--Schmidt criterion under those
assumptions alone~\cite{shimomura1977linear}. The i.i.d. structure lets us go
further: it identifies the coordinate symmetry approached by remote rows,
and the local Hellinger geometry forces their errors to be square summable.
This gives both necessity and sufficiency for arbitrary bounded invertible
mixing. The classification complements constructions of non-Gaussian
quasi-invariant measures \cite{feldman1961examples,takahashi1975quasi} by
determining the linear transformations available to one fixed i.i.d. law.

The Fisher assumptions control the local cost of perturbing the density.
For translations, Shepp relates this cost to regularity of the square-root
density \cite{shepp1965translate}; Euclidean and Lie-group perturbations
lead to Fisher information as the local metric
\cite{steele1986fisher,marques1992quasi}. Our proof uses this local metric
to obtain a classification for arbitrary bounded invertible mixing.
In the Gaussian setting, translation and nonlinear transformation laws
are developed by Cameron--Martin and Ramer
\cite{cameron1944transformations,ramer1974nonlinear}; here the issue is the
rigidity imposed on linear mixing by a fixed non-Gaussian product.

Classical independence-of-linear-forms theorems
\cite{kac1939characterization,darmois1953analyse,skitovich1954linear,kagan1973characterization}
characterize Gaussian laws through independence of suitable linear forms,
while independent component analysis identifies non-Gaussian mixing matrices
up to scaling and permutation~\cite{comon1994independent}. These results
explain why coordinate permutations appear among the exact symmetries.
Measure equivalence allows dependent outputs, so our necessity proof must
recover coordinate structure without assuming that the mixed variables are
independent.

\subsection{How tails and support change the answer}

A large input can move many outputs at once. For stable coordinates this
event is frequent enough to change the cost of mixing. The classification
in \cref{thm:complete-criterion-symmetric-stable-coordinates} replaces the
Hilbert--Schmidt condition by
\[
 \sum_i|m_{ii}-1|^2
 +\sum_j\left(\sum_{i\ne j}|m_{ij}|^2\right)^{\alpha/2}<\infty.
\]
Here \(0<\alpha<2\) is the stable index and \(M=TQ^{-1}\) is the matrix
after removing a signed permutation; the theorem specifies the compatible
row-series action. Scales retain a quadratic cost. An off-diagonal column
records how far one input moves the other coordinates, and its
\(\ell^2\)-length is charged to the power \(\alpha\). This distinguishes
many independent small shears from a single source mixed into many outputs.

Zeros of the density create a different obstruction. A contraction of one
coordinate can leave part of the original support with no preimage, even
though its operator differs from the identity by rank one. Theorem~
\ref{thm:linear-equivalence-group} separates this loss of support from the
accumulated Hellinger cost: Hilbert--Schmidt proximity controls the latter,
while the forward and inverse row sums must avoid the zeros of the density.
For bounded or one-sided laws, the support alone forces the explicit
permutation classifications of \cref{thm:bounded-and-half-line-classification}.

\subsection{From a rotation to infinite-dimensional rigidity}

The first question is how to detect mixing without demanding higher
moments. Covariance cannot see a rotation, so we test it with bounded
complex exponentials. Centering the two factors removes their marginal
responses and leaves
\[
 R(\xi,\eta)=\eta\phi'(\xi)\phi(\eta)
              -\xi\phi(\xi)\phi'(\eta),
 \qquad \phi(u)=\E e^{iuX}.
\]
If every such response vanished, fixing one frequency would give a linear
ODE forcing \(\phi\) to be Gaussian. Geometrically, the weighted Fourier jet
\(u\mapsto(u\phi(u),\phi'(u))\) would lie in a line. A non-Gaussian law
therefore supplies two independent Fourier directions. We use them to build
tests dual to every matrix tangent.

An infinitesimal response is not yet a uniform lower bound. As the dimension
grows, errors from many small entries could overwhelm it. We control these
errors by following the square-root density along an exponential path and
grouping terms according to their shared coordinates. Independence then
turns the sums into Frobenius contractions. This gives a fixed local
Hellinger bound in every dimension.

The remaining difficulty is global: why should an equivalent image law be
close to a coordinate symmetry at all? Absolute continuity makes its remote
marginals approach the original coordinate law. A characteristic-function
argument shows that each corresponding row must approach an allowed signed
coordinate. This identifies the axes, but says nothing yet about the sum of
the errors.

To force summability, suppose that a fixed small amount of error could
always be collected in the tail. Exchange those coordinates with later ones
whose matching errors are negligible, and exchange the corresponding input
coordinates as well. Compose the input exchange with the output exchange
transported back through \(T\). The resulting map differs from the identity
by precisely the amount of matching error that the local lower bound detects.
Yet absolute continuity makes every sufficiently
remote exchange invisible in Hellinger distance. The contradiction forces
the matching errors to be square summable. Finite-coordinate approximation
proves the converse, and translation ergodicity supplies the
equivalence--singularity alternative.

We use \(A^*\) for the adjoint, \(P_n\) for coordinate projection on \(H\),
and \(\|\cdot\|_F\) for the Frobenius norm.
