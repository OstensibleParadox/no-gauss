\section{Introduction and Main Results}
\label{sec:introduction-and-main-results}

\subsection{The question and answer}
\label{sec:question-answer}

Fix one non-Gaussian coordinate law \(\rho\) and the i.i.d. product
\(\mu=\rho^{\otimes\N}\).
For which bounded invertible linear maps \(T\) on \(\ell^2\) is the
induced image law equivalent to \(\mu\)?
Kakutani's product criterion answers coordinatewise perturbations, but it
does not survive bounded invertible linear mixing: the transformed coordinates cease to be
independent and the Hellinger affinity no longer tensorizes.
The obstruction is not visible from any fixed finite window, since every
fixed finite output marginal can have an a.e.-positive density even when
the full image law is singular.
Under the finite-variance and Fisher hypotheses of the main theorem, the
continuous Gaussian symmetry collapses to the allowed signed coordinate
permutations, and the surviving continuous thickness is exactly
Hilbert--Schmidt.
Equivalently, the projected Hellinger energies of the finite output
marginals are bounded exactly on that equivalence branch.
This conclusion is not a consequence of the a.e.-positive-density assumption alone.
For standard symmetric \(\alpha\)-stable coordinates with
\(0<\alpha<2\), the density is everywhere positive but the exact criterion
is governed by an anisotropic mixing cost: diagonal errors are square summable and
the off-diagonal input-column norms are \(\alpha\)-summable.
When a finite-variance density satisfies the Sobolev assumptions but has
zeros, the Hilbert--Schmidt condition must be supplemented by the zero-set
row condition: the rows of both \(T\) and \(T^{-1}\) must avoid the zero set of the
one-dimensional density.

\subsection{Historical context: from Kakutani to bounded invertible linear mixing}
\label{sec:history}

Kakutani's theorem turns equivalence of infinite products into a scalar
summability problem.
The test is the product of one-coordinate Hellinger affinities, positive
exactly on the equivalence branch and zero exactly on the singular
branch~\cite{kakutani1948equivalence}.
Sharp criteria for sequences of Euclidean and, more generally, Lie-group
perturbations retain this product structure and identify Fisher information
as the local Hellinger metric
\cite{steele1986fisher,marques1992quasi}.

The Gaussian law conceals the obstruction created by bounded invertible
linear mixing.
For the standard Gaussian product, every orthogonal operator on \(\ell^2\)
has a measurable row-series realization and preserves the product law.
Translation and linear measure-class changes are governed by the
Cameron--Martin and Feldman--H\'ajek theories, with explicit Gaussian
density criteria developed by Shepp and nonlinear extensions by Ramer
\cite{cameron1944transformations,feldman1958equivalence,hajek1958property,
shepp1966radon,ramer1974nonlinear,bogachev1998gaussian}.
A non-Gaussian product behaves differently: exact independence rigidifies
the orthogonal group to coordinate permutations and to those coordinate
signs already preserving the one-dimensional law.
Non-Gaussian quasi-invariant measures on Hilbert and linear topological
spaces have long been known
\cite{feldman1961examples,takahashi1975quasi}; the problem here is the
complete measure-class classification for bounded invertible linear mixing of one fixed
i.i.d. product law.

\subsection{The phase transition: continuous Gaussian symmetry and discrete rigidity}
\label{sec:phase-transition}

Let \(X\) have a centered probability law \(\rho\) with finite first moment
and characteristic function \(\phi(u)=\E e^{iuX}\).
The rotational Fourier defect used below has a specific job: it turns the
dependence created by a small non-Gaussian rotation into a bounded
observable whose strength does not deteriorate with the ambient dimension.
Define the rotational Fourier defect by
\begin{equation}
 R(\xi,\eta)=\eta\phi'(\xi)\phi(\eta)-\xi\phi(\xi)\phi'(\eta).
 \label{eq:fourier-defect}
\end{equation}
It is the first variation of the expectation of a product of centered
complex exponentials under a planar rotation.  Its determinant representation
uses the weighted Fourier jet, the explicit curve
\begin{equation}
 u\longmapsto\bigl(u\phi(u),\phi'(u)\bigr)\in\mathbb C^2.
 \label{eq:weighted-fourier-jet}
\end{equation}
Up to sign, \(R(\xi,\eta)\) is the determinant of the two vectors
on this curve at \(\xi\) and \(\eta\).
Hence zero rotational Fourier defect means that the curve lies in one fixed complex line
through the origin.
The resulting global linear ODE has only Gaussian characteristic functions
as solutions.

Under the finite-second-moment and Sobolev assumptions below, a finite set
of frequencies detects every infinitesimal matrix direction for a
non-Gaussian coordinate law.
The corresponding Hellinger orbit is uniformly coercive in Frobenius norm
near the exact linear stabilizer, independently of dimension.
In that finite-variance regime, the surviving continuous thickness is
exactly Hilbert--Schmidt: equivalence holds precisely for
Hilbert--Schmidt perturbations of coordinate permutations whose signs
preserve \(\rho\).  The symmetric \(\alpha\)-stable regime has the same exact
linear stabilizer but a different, anisotropic accumulation law, as shown in
\cref{sec:symmetric-stable-products-and-anisotropic-geometry}.

\subsection{Main theorem: equivalence, projected Hellinger energy, and the exact linear stabilizer}
\label{sec:main-results}

The main finite-variance theorem has a simple nontechnical content.
For coordinate laws satisfying \eqref{eq:main-assumptions}, bounded invertible linear mixing
preserves the product measure class exactly when it differs by
Hilbert--Schmidt size from the exact linear stabilizer.
The projected Hellinger energies defined below are not heuristic proxies for
this condition; their boundedness is an equivalent diagnostic.
An everywhere-positive density alone does not imply this Hilbert--Schmidt criterion: the
standard symmetric \(\alpha\)-stable laws have everywhere positive
densities, but their infinite-variance classification requires the stronger
anisotropic mixing cost in \eqref{eq:stable-mixing-cost}.

We now state the assumptions used for the theorem on bounded invertible linear mixing.
Suppose \(\rho(dx)=f(x)\,dx\), put \(g=\sqrt f\), and
assume
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 f>0\quad\text{Lebesgue-almost everywhere},\qquad
 g\in W^{1,2}(\R),\qquad xg'\in L^2(\R),\\
 \E X=0,\qquad \E X^2=1,\qquad
 f\ne(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/2}.
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:main-assumptions}
\end{equation}
All derivatives are weak derivatives.
Cusps are allowed.
No moment of order greater than two is assumed.
The two Sobolev conditions are finite location and scale Fisher information.

Put \(H=\ell^2(\N;\R)\) and
\(\mu=\rho^{\otimes\N}\) on the product Borel space \(\R^{\N}\),
and \(\mu_n=\rho^{\otimes n}\).  The
space \(H\) indexes coefficients.  Write \(\mathcal B(H)\) for its
bounded linear operators, \(\GL(H)\) for the invertible members, and
\((e_i)_{i\ge1}\) for the standard coordinate basis.
A sample from \(\mu\) is not asserted to belong to \(H\).
Define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal P=\{Q:{}&(Qx)_i=\varepsilon_i x_{\pi(i)},\quad
       \pi\text{ a permutation of }\N,\\
       &\varepsilon_i\in\{-1,1\},\quad\Law(\varepsilon_iX)=\rho
       \text{ for every }i\}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:allowed-permutations}
\end{equation}
Write \(\mathcal P^{\pm}\) for the full signed-permutation group.
Thus \(\mathcal P=\mathcal P^{\pm}\) when \(\rho\) is symmetric, whereas
\(\mathcal P\) is the ordinary permutation group when \(\rho\) is not
symmetric.
This definition also applies when \(\rho\) has no density.
For a bounded linear operator \(T\) on \(H\), let \(\Phi_T\) be the
measurable row-series realization
constructed in \cref{lem:measurable-row-series-realization}.
Write \(\nu_T=(\Phi_T)_\#\mu\).

For probability measures \(\lambda,\nu\), set
\begin{equation}
 \Aff(\lambda,\nu)
 =\int\sqrt{\frac{d\lambda}{dm}\frac{d\nu}{dm}}\,dm,
 \qquad
 h(\lambda,\nu)^2=2-2\Aff(\lambda,\nu),
 \label{eq:affinity-convention}
\end{equation}
where \(m\) dominates both measures.  We use the same notation for
densities and their probability measures.  Let \(P_n:H\to H\) be the
orthogonal projection onto the first \(n\) coordinate vectors, and let
\(\pi_n:\R^{\N}\to\R^n\) extract the first \(n\) coordinates.
Write \(\mathcal S_2(H)\) for the Hilbert--Schmidt ideal.  Define the
projected Hellinger energy by
\begin{equation}
 E_n(T)=-2\log\Aff\bigl(\mu_n,(\pi_n)_\#\nu_T\bigr).
 \label{eq:projected-hellinger-energy}
\end{equation}
These are actual output marginals, not laws obtained from principal matrix
corners.  Thus \(E_n\) keeps the finite evidence produced by the actual
output rows and asks whether that evidence remains uniformly bounded
as the window grows.

\begin{theorem}[Complete criterion for bounded invertible linear mixing]
\label{thm:measure-equivalence}
Under \eqref{eq:main-assumptions}, for every \(T\in\GL(H)\),
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\begin{aligned}
 \nu_T\sim\mu
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists Q\in\mathcal P:\ TQ^{-1}-I\in\mathcal S_2(H)\\
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sup_nE_n(T)<\infty.
 \end{aligned}}
 \label{eq:measure-equivalence}
\end{equation}
If these conditions fail, then \(\nu_T\perp\mu\) and
\(E_n(T)\uparrow\infty\).  The exact linear stabilizer in \(\GL(H)\)
is \(\mathcal P\): \(\nu_T=\mu\) if and only if \(T\in\mathcal P\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Finite-coordinate approximants recover the likelihood ratio]
\label{prop:finite-coordinate-approximants-recover-likelihood-ratio}
Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:measure-equivalence}, suppose
\(\nu_T\sim\mu\).
For any admissible \(Q\), put \(K=TQ^{-1}-I\) and
\(T_n=I+P_nKP_n\).  Then \(T_n\) is eventually invertible and
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\nu_{T_n}}{d\mu}\longrightarrow\frac{d\nu_{TQ^{-1}}}{d\mu}
       \quad\text{in }L^1(\mu),
 \qquad
 \sqrt{\frac{d\nu_{T_n}}{d\mu}}
       \longrightarrow
 \sqrt{\frac{d\nu_{TQ^{-1}}}{d\mu}}
       \quad\text{in }L^2(\mu).
 \label{eq:likelihood-convergence}
\end{equation}
Since \(\nu_Q=\mu\), the limiting law is also \(\nu_T\).
Moreover, the finite-marginal likelihood ratios and their reciprocals are
uniformly integrable in their respective reference measures.
\end{proposition}

\Cref{prop:finite-coordinate-approximants-recover-likelihood-ratio} is the analytic bookkeeping
behind the criterion: finite-dimensional likelihood computations do not
merely approximate cylinder probabilities, but converge to the actual
infinite-dimensional likelihood on the equivalence branch.

The theorem contains the global energy formulation through
\(\sup_nE_n(T)\).
The rotational Fourier defect is not an additional scalar condition on \(T\).
It is the calibration mechanism.
It turns small Hellinger displacement into squared Frobenius displacement.
This distinction keeps the one-dimensional Gaussian characterization separate
from the operator-valued equivalence criterion.

Two refinements identify the assumptions needed for the stabilizer and
for orthogonal transformations.

\begin{corollary}[Moment-only stabilizer]
\label{cor:moment-only-stabilizer}
If \(\rho\) is non-Gaussian and centered with variance one,
then the exact linear stabilizer of
\(\mu=\rho^{\otimes\N}\) is \(\mathcal P\).  No density
or Fisher information assumption is needed.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Equivalence under orthogonal transformations]
\label{cor:orthogonal-equivalence}
If \(f>0\) almost everywhere, \(\sqrt f\in W^{1,2}(\R)\), and \(f\) is
non-Gaussian and centered with variance one, then every orthogonal \(U\) obeys
\[
 \nu_U\sim\mu
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 U-Q\in\mathcal S_2\quad\text{for some }Q\in\mathcal P.
\]
Thus scale Fisher information is needed for bounded invertible linear mixing but not for
the orthogonal subproblem.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Result map}
\label{sec:result-map}

The classification separates metric accumulation from the zero-set row
condition.
The following map records the strongest conclusion in each regime and the
reason that conclusion matters.

The first row is the finite-variance main theorem.
The second isolates the common situation in which the operator is already
orthogonal, so the scale score never enters.
The third is the infinite-variance stable classification.  Its anisotropic
mixing cost records the heavy-tailed source coordinate shared by all
receiving rows, and the corresponding anisotropic stable Hellinger
coercivity quantifies that cost in finite dimensions.
The fourth is the replacement by the zero-set row condition; it says exactly
what null-set obstruction must be checked in addition to Hilbert--Schmidt
size.
The fifth shows that in one-sided and bounded geometries the zero-set row
condition can be so rigid that the continuous Hilbert--Schmidt neighborhood
disappears.

\subsection{Organization and notation}
\label{sec:organization}

\Cref{sec:rotational-fourier-defect-and-gaussian-characterization} rules out
continuous non-Gaussian rotation blind spots.
\Cref{sec:finite-dimensional-hellinger-coercivity} turns that rotational Fourier defect
into dimension-free local Hellinger coercivity and then states the finite-dimensional global form.
\Cref{sec:measurable-realization-and-dichotomy} builds the measurable
row-series realization and shows that the projected Hellinger energy is an exact
equivalence diagnostic.
\Cref{sec:finite-variance-equivalence-and-global-rigidity} proves sufficiency by
constructing convergent likelihood ratios and proves necessity by showing that
non-Hilbert--Schmidt tail errors cannot be hidden from local coercivity and
invariance under finite tail permutations.
\Cref{sec:symmetric-stable-products-and-anisotropic-geometry} classifies
standard symmetric \(\alpha\)-stable products and identifies their
anisotropic mixing cost, with diagonal-square and off-diagonal-column terms.
\Cref{sec:zero-set-row-condition-and-linear-equivalence} gives the
extension by the zero-set row condition, including boundary contrasts and the bounded-support and
half-line classifications.
\Cref{sec:scope-of-the-finite-variance-hypotheses} finally separates the
roles of the finite-variance assumptions and records examples at their
natural boundary.

We write \(A^*\) for the adjoint, including the transpose of a real
matrix, and \(\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}\) for the operator norm.
The Frobenius inner product is
\(\langle A,B\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)\), with norm \(\|A\|_F\).
The law \(\rho\) remains fixed unless a different law is explicitly specified.
